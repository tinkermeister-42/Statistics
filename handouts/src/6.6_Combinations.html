Pick 3 students from 5 (A, B, C, D, E) to be on a committee. All 3 have the same job. Choosing A, B, C is the same committee as choosing C, A, B. Order does not matter.

Count as if order mattered:${}_5P_3 = 5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 60$
Each committee of 3 was counted $3! = 6$ times:ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA
Divide:$60 \div 6 = \mathbf{10}$ committees

Combination formula:    ${}_nC_r = \left.\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}\right. = \dfrac{n!}{r!\,\mathopen{(}n - r\mathclose{)}!}$    choose $r$ things from $n$ things

${}_nC_r$ and $\left.\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}\right.$ are both read “$n$ choose $r$.” Some books write $C(n, r)$.

Example:    ${}_5C_3 = \dfrac{5!}{3!\,2!} = \dfrac{120}{6 \cdot 2} = 10$

Permutation or combination? Ask: If I switch the order, is it a different result?

Order matters: permutationOrder does not matter: combination
President, vice president, secretary (different jobs)A committee of 3 (same job)
1st, 2nd, 3rd placePicking 3 winners of the same prize
Arranging books on a shelfChoosing 3 books to take on a trip

Handshakes. 10 people each shake hands once with everyone else. A handshake is a pair, and Ana-Ben is the same as Ben-Ana:    ${}_{10}C_2 = \dfrac{10 \cdot 9}{2} = 45$ handshakes.

  1. Choose 3 pizza toppings from 10.  {{a}}Combination. ${}_{10}C_3 = 120${{/a}}

3. 12 people meet. Each person shakes hands once with every other person. How many handshakes?
{{a}}${}_{12}C_2 = \frac{12 \cdot 11}{2} = 66${{/a}}

4. A restaurant has 8 toppings. A pizza has 2 different toppings. How many different 2-topping pizzas are there?
{{a}}${}_8C_2 = \frac{8 \cdot 7}{2} = 28${{/a}}
5. Compare ${}_{10}P_3 = 720$ and ${}_{10}C_3 = 120$.
  1. Why is ${}_{10}P_3$ bigger?  {{a}}It counts each group of 3 once for every order.{{/a}}
  2. What number do you divide by to get from 720 to 120? Why?  {{a}}$3! = 6$. Each group of 3 can be ordered 6 ways.{{/a}}
6. A hand of 5 cards is dealt from a deck of 52 cards. Use a calculator. How many different hands are there?
{{a}}${}_{52}C_5 = 2{,}598{,}960${{/a}}
7. A student says, “Choosing 2 of 6 students to clean the board is a permutation, because there are 2 people.” Do you agree? Explain.
{{a}}No. Both students do the same job, so the order does not matter. It is a combination: ${}_6C_2 = 15$.{{/a}}
