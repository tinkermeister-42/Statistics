The counting principle. If one choice has $m$ options and a second choice has $n$ options, there are $m \cdot n$ ways to make both choices.

Example: 3 shirts and 4 pairs of pants make $3 \cdot 4 = 12$ outfits.

Arranging. In how many ways can 4 students sit in 4 chairs in a row?

$4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24$ ways

Factorial:   $n! = n \cdot (n - 1) \cdot (n - 2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1$    read “$n$ factorial”

$4! = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24$    $1! = 1$    $0! = 1$ (there is exactly one way to arrange nothing)

Permutations. A permutation is an arrangement where order matters.

Example: A club of 10 students picks a president, a vice president, and a secretary. How many ways?

$10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$ ways.   (Ana as president and Ben as vice president is different from Ben as president and Ana as vice president.)

Permutation formula:    ${}_nP_r = \dfrac{n!}{(n - r)!}$    arrange $r$ things chosen from $n$ things

${}_{10}P_3 = \dfrac{10!}{7!} =$ 10 · 9 · 8 · 7 · 6 ⋯ 17 · 6 ⋯ 1 $= 720$

${}_nP_r$ is read “$n$ permute $r$.” Some books write $P(n, r)$.

Repeats allowed? 3-letter codes from 26 letters:

No repeats:$26 \cdot 25 \cdot 24 = 15{,}600$   (a permutation)
Repeats allowed:$26 \cdot 26 \cdot 26 = 26^3 = 17{,}576$   (not a permutation; use the counting principle)
