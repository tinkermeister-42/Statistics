One study, every tool. A school has 800 students. A company says its new homework app raises quiz scores. The principal wants to find out if that is true.

5.1 Who is in the study?

The population is the whole group. The sample is the part we collect data from.

 Population: all 800 students at the school.
Idea 1: Post a link: “Did the app help you?” 150 students answer. This is voluntary response bias. Students with strong feelings answer most.
Idea 2: Compare students who chose the app with students who did not. This is an observational study. Students who chose the app may already work harder.

5.2 Choose the sample.

Use chance.
Stratified: a few from every group.
Cluster: everyone from a few groups.

 Randomly pick 10 students from each grade, 9 to 12. The sample is 40 students.
This is a stratified sample. Every grade is in it, and chance picks the students.

5.3 Run an experiment.

Random assignment makes the groups alike. The control group does not get the new treatment.

 {{fig Unit_5/Review_app_design}} Explanatory variable: app or paper.   Response variable: quiz score.
Group 2 is the control group. Both groups get the same homework and take the same quiz.

5.4 What can it prove?

Random sample: we can generalize.
Random assignment: we can say cause.
Less than 5% by chance: statistically significant.

 App group: $\bar{x} = 82$.  Paper group: $\bar{x} = 74$.  Difference: $82 - 74 = 8$ points.
Shuffle the 40 scores into two random groups of 20. Repeat 100 times. {{fig Unit_5/Review_app_simulation}} Only 2 of the 100 shuffles gave 8 or more (2%). The result is statistically significant.
Random sample: we can generalize to all 800 students.
Random assignment: the app caused the higher scores.

A bigger sample does not fix bias. If 1,000 people choose to answer a poll, it is still a voluntary response sample. Only chance makes a sample represent the population.

2. I name the type of bias in a sample or a question, and I decide if results can be generalized to a population.
Name the type of bias. Explain how it could change the results.
  1. A news website asks readers to click yes or no: “Should phones be allowed in class?”
    {{a}}Voluntary response bias. Readers choose to vote. People with strong feelings vote most, so the result may not match all readers.{{/a}}
  2. To learn what music students at his school like, Sam asks his friends at lunch.
    {{a}}Convenience. Sam's friends may like the same music as Sam, so the results may not match the school.{{/a}}
  3. A school wants to know how many students have a library card. It surveys a random sample of the students who visited the library this year.
    {{a}}Undercoverage. Students who never visit the library are left out. They are less likely to have a card, so the percent will look too high.{{/a}}
  4. A store mails a survey to 1,000 randomly chosen customers. Only 150 send it back.
    {{a}}Nonresponse bias. The 150 who answer may feel more strongly (very happy or very upset) than the 850 who do not. Voluntary response: partial credit. The cue is “randomly chosen”, so the answer is nonresponse.{{/a}}
  5. A survey asks, “Do you agree that the new lunch menu, which students love, should stay?”
    {{a}}Response bias. The words “which students love” push people to say yes.{{/a}}
  6. A teacher asks each student, in front of the whole class, “Did you finish all of your homework this week?”
    {{a}}Response bias. The setting pushes students to say yes, because the class is listening.{{/a}}

Can the results be generalized to the population? Explain.

  1. To learn how students get to school, Omar asks 50 students who are waiting at the bus stop.
    {{a}}No. Everyone at the bus stop rides the bus. Students who walk or get a ride are left out, so the sample does not represent the school.{{/a}}
  2. A school numbers its 1,200 students. A random number generator picks 100 of them. The school asks them how much time they spend on homework.
    {{a}}Yes, to all 1,200 students. The sample was chosen at random from the whole school, so it should represent the school well.{{/a}}
{{break}}

A town has 6,000 residents. 4,500 live in town, and 1,500 live on farms outside town. The town wants to ask 120 residents about a new road.

  1. Describe a good sampling method. Name it, and explain why it is a good choice.
    {{a}}Stratified sample. Split the residents into town residents and farm residents. $\frac{120}{6{,}000} = 2\%$. Randomly pick $2\%$ of each group: $0.02 \times 4{,}500 = 90$ town residents and $0.02 \times 1{,}500 = 30$ farm residents. Farm residents may use the road in a different way, so both groups are in the sample in the right numbers. Also accept, with a reason: a stratified sample with equal numbers from each group, a simple random sample, or a systematic sample from the full list of 6,000.{{/a}}
  2. Someone says to ask 120 people at the grocery store in town. What is wrong with this method?
    {{a}}It is a convenience sample. People who live on farms, or shop somewhere else, are less likely to be there. The sample may not represent the town.{{/a}}
  1. A bus company has 30 buses with 40 seats each. It wants to check 120 seats for damage. Describe a fast way to choose the seats. Name the method.
    {{a}}Cluster sample. Randomly pick 3 buses and check all 40 seats in each. $3 \times 40 = 120$ seats. Workers only go to 3 buses.{{/a}}
  2. A teacher wants to choose 4 of her 28 students to test a new website. Describe how to choose a simple random sample.
    {{a}}Number the students 01 to 28. Use a random digit table (or a random number generator) to pick 4 numbers. Skip numbers over 28 and repeats.{{/a}}

5.3 - Experiments
5. I name the subjects, treatments, explanatory variable, and response variable in an experiment, and I explain why random assignment matters.
Does a new kind of soil help pepper plants grow taller? 36 pepper plants are randomly split into two groups of 18. One group grows in the new soil. The other group grows in regular soil. After 8 weeks, a class measures the height of each plant.
  1. What are the subjects?
    {{a}}The 36 pepper plants{{/a}}
  2. What are the treatments?
    {{a}}New soil and regular soil{{/a}}
  3. What is the explanatory variable?
    {{a}}The type of soil{{/a}}
  4. What is the response variable?
    {{a}}The height of each plant after 8 weeks{{/a}}
  5. What is the control group?
    {{a}}The 18 plants in regular soil{{/a}}
  6. Why split the plants into groups at random?
    {{a}}So the two groups are alike. The strongest plants do not all end up in one group. Then a difference in height comes from the soil, not from the plants.{{/a}}

A phone company tests a new battery saving setting. 50 phones of the same model are randomly split into two groups of 25. One group uses the new setting. The other group uses the normal setting. Each phone plays the same video until the battery dies. The company records how many hours each battery lasts.

  1. What are the subjects?
    {{a}}The 50 phones{{/a}}
  2. What is the explanatory variable?
    {{a}}The setting (new or normal){{/a}}
  3. What is the response variable?
    {{a}}How many hours the battery lasts{{/a}}
  4. Instead, the company puts the 25 newest phones in the new setting group. Why is this a problem?
    {{a}}Newer batteries may last longer anyway. Then we cannot tell if the setting or the newer batteries caused the longer time. Random assignment makes the groups alike.{{/a}}

6. I explain control groups, placebos, and blinding, and I describe completely randomized, randomized block, and matched pairs designs.
A company says its new “study tea” helps students do better on math tests. 60 student volunteers are randomly split into two groups of 30. One group drinks study tea. The other group drinks a plain tea that looks and tastes the same. Then every student takes the same math test. A teacher grades the tests.
  1. What is the control group?  {{a}}The 30 students who drink the plain tea{{/a}}
  2. The plain tea is a placebo. What is a placebo?
    {{a}}A fake treatment that looks real.{{/a}}
  3. Why give the control group a placebo, and not nothing at all?
    {{a}}Students may do better just because they think they got study tea (the placebo effect). With a placebo, both groups think they may have it. So an extra difference comes from the study tea itself.{{/a}}
  4. Why should the students not know which tea they drank?
    {{a}}If they know they got study tea, they may feel sure and try harder. The placebo effect could change their scores.{{/a}}
  5. Why should the teacher who grades the tests not know?
    {{a}}She might, without meaning to, grade the study tea group more kindly.{{/a}}
  6. Neither the students nor the teacher knows who drank which tea. What is this called?
    {{a}}Double-blind{{/a}}
  7. Is this experiment a completely randomized design, a randomized block design, or a matched pairs design? Explain.
    {{a}}Completely randomized. Each student gets only one treatment, and chance alone decides which one. The students were not split into blocks first.{{/a}}
  8. Describe how to test study tea with a matched pairs design.
    {{a}}Each student takes two math tests of the same difficulty on two days: once after study tea and once after plain tea. A coin flip decides which tea comes first. Compare each student's two scores. Also accept: pair students with similar scores (for example, on a pretest), then flip a coin in each pair to choose who drinks study tea.{{/a}}
  9. Suppose each student drinks both teas, on two different days. Why flip a coin to decide which tea comes first?
    {{a}}Students may do better on the second test (practice) or worse (tired). The coin keeps that from always helping the same tea.{{/a}}
  10. Why is a matched pairs design a good choice here?
    {{a}}Each student is compared to themselves. So differences in math skill from student to student do not matter. (If you paired similar students: each pair has similar skill, so skill differences matter less.){{/a}}
{{break}}
  1. 24 of the 60 students take Algebra 1 and 36 take Algebra 2. Students in Algebra 2 may score higher on a math test. Describe how to test study tea with a randomized block design.
    {{a}}Make two blocks: the Algebra 1 students and the Algebra 2 students. In each block, randomly assign half to study tea and half to plain tea (12 and 12 in Algebra 1, 18 and 18 in Algebra 2). Compare the two teas inside each block.{{/a}}
  2. Why split the students by class before assigning the teas?
    {{a}}The class could change test scores. Splitting first gives each tea the same mix of Algebra 1 and Algebra 2 students. Comparing inside each block keeps that difference out of the result.{{/a}}
{{break}}

8. I use a simulation to decide if a result is statistically significant, and I find mistakes in conclusions about studies.
A coach tests a new jump training. 24 players are randomly split into two groups of 12. After 4 weeks, the coach measures how high each player jumps. Each dot is the difference between the group means in one random shuffle of the 24 jumps, when the training does nothing.
{{fig Unit_5/Review_jump_simulation left}}
  1. The training group's mean jump was 5 cm higher. How many of the 100 shuffles gave a difference of 5 or more?
    {{a}}$1 + 1 = 2$ shuffles{{/a}}
  2. What percent of the shuffles is that?  {{a}}$\frac{2}{100} = 2\%${{/a}}
  3. Is a 5 cm difference statistically significant? Explain.
    {{a}}Yes. Chance gave a difference this big only 2% of the time. That is less than 5%.{{/a}}
  1. Another team did the same experiment. Its training group jumped 2 cm higher. How many shuffles gave 2 or more? Is 2 cm statistically significant?
    {{a}}$12 + 8 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 27$ shuffles (27%). No. Chance gives a difference this big often.{{/a}}
  2. What is the smallest whole number of cm that would be statistically significant? Explain.
    {{a}}5 cm. 5 or more happened 2 times (2%), which is less than 5%. 4 or more happened $5 + 1 + 1 = 7$ times (7%), which is not.{{/a}}

    {{a}}The volunteers are not a random sample of all music students. We can only say Plan B caused faster learning for these 36 volunteers.{{/a}}
  3. In a randomized experiment, Group 1 scored 3 points higher than Group 2. A simulation shows chance gives a difference of 3 or more 35 times out of 100. Conclusion: “The treatment for Group 1 works better.”
    {{a}}It is not statistically significant. Chance gives 3 or more 35% of the time.{{/a}}
