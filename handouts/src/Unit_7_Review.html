7.1 Theoretical or empirical?
Theoretical: from equally likely outcomes.
Empirical: from data. Divide each count by the total.

The 4 parts are equally likely, so the table above is theoretical.

The booth counted 100 spins: $\$0$ 52 times, $\$1$ 23 times, $\$5$ 25 times. This is the empirical distribution:

$P(0) = \dfrac{52}{100} = 0.52$,    $P(1) = 0.23$,    $P(5) = 0.25$

It is close to the theoretical one, but not the same.

7.2 Find the expected value.
$E(X) = \Sigma\, x \cdot P(x)$
Multiply each value by its probability. Then add. $E(X)$ is also the mean of $X$, written $\mu_X$.
$x$015
$P(x)$0.50.250.25
$x \cdot P(x)$00.251.25

$E(X) = 0 + 0.25 + 1.25 = \$1.50$

Over many spins, a spin pays about $\$1.50$ on average. No single spin pays $\$1.50$.

7.3 Find the expected payoff.
Net gain $=$ what you win $-$ what you paid.
A game is fair if the expected payoff is 0.

A spin costs $\$2$.

OutcomeNet gain $x$$P(x)$$x \cdot P(x)$
Win $\$0$$0 - 2 = -2$0.5$-1$
Win $\$1$$1 - 2 = -1$0.25$-0.25$
Win $\$5$$5 - 2 = 3$0.250.75

Expected payoff $= -1 - 0.25 + 0.75 = -\$0.50$

On average, a player loses 50 cents a spin. The game is not fair. The booth keeps that money for the school. A spin that cost $\$1.50$ would be fair.

Expected value is a long-run average. It tells you what happens over many tries. It does not tell you what happens on one try. For a decision, also think about risk: can you pay for the worst outcome?

    {{a}}No plan.  $\$29 - \$21 = \$8$ cheaper on average.{{/a}}

    {{a}}They could not pay a sudden $\$350$ bill. The plan removes that risk.{{/a}}
