Probability Symbols

SymbolRead it asMeaning
$P(A)$“the probability of $A$”How likely $A$ is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
$P(\text{not } A)$“the probability of not $A$”The complement: $A$ does not happen. Some books write $P(A^C)$.
$A \cup B$“$A$ union $B$” or “$A$ or $B$”$A$ happens, or $B$ happens, or both.
$A \cap B$“$A$ intersect $B$” or “$A$ and $B$”$A$ and $B$ both happen (the overlap).
$P(A \mid B)$“the probability of $A$ given $B$”The probability of $A$ when you know $B$ happened. The bar $\mid$ means “given.”

Probability Rules

RuleFormulaWhen to useExample
Probability $P(A) =$
$\dfrac{\text{number of ways } A \text{ can happen}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$		 Every outcome is equally likely. 3 red and 7 blue marbles. Pick one. $P(\text{red}) = \frac{3}{10}$
Complement $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$
$P(\text{at least one wrong})$
$= 1 - P(\text{all right})$		 “Not $A$.” Also “at least one” questions. $P(\text{not red}) = 1 - \frac{3}{10} = \frac{7}{10}$
Guess on 3 questions with 4 choices each: $1 - \frac{1}{64} = \frac{63}{64}$
Addition rule $P(A \cup B) =$
$P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$		 “Or.” Subtract the overlap, because it was counted twice. 30 students: 12 play soccer ($S$), 10 play basketball ($B$), 4 play both.
$P(S \cup B) = \frac{12}{30} + \frac{10}{30} - \frac{4}{30} = \frac{18}{30}$
Mutually exclusive $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$
$P(A \cap B) = 0$		 “Or,” when $A$ and $B$ cannot both happen. Roll a number cube.
$P(1 \cup 2) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$
Multiplication rule $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$ “And,” for independent events. Flip a coin and roll a number cube.
$P(\text{heads} \cap 6) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{12}$
General multiplication rule $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)$ “And,” for dependent events. 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Draw 2. Keep the first one out.
$P(\text{red, red}) = \frac{5}{8} \cdot \frac{4}{7} = \frac{5}{14}$
Conditional probability $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$
In a table:
$P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{\text{number in } A \text{ and } B}{\text{number in } B}$		 You already know $B$ happened. Only look at the $B$ group. 60 students: 30 play a sport, 20 have a job, 8 do both.
$P(\text{job} \mid \text{sport}) = \frac{8}{30} \approx 0.27$
$P(\text{sport} \mid \text{job}) = \frac{8}{20} = 0.40$
Checking independence $A$ and $B$ are independent if $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$. Knowing $B$ does not change the probability of $A$. $P(\text{job}) = \frac{20}{60} \approx 0.33$, but $P(\text{job} \mid \text{sport}) \approx 0.27$. Not independent.

Independent: one happening does not change the probability of the other, as in drawing with replacement. Dependent: one happening changes it, as in drawing without replacement.

Simulations

IdeaWhat it meansExample
Theoretical probability Found by reasoning about equally likely outcomes. Flip a coin. $P(\text{heads}) = \frac{1}{2}$
Experimental probability Found by doing trials: $\dfrac{\text{number of times it happened}}{\text{number of trials}}$ 20 flips, 12 heads: $\frac{12}{20} = 0.6$
Simulation Use random numbers to act out a chance event many times.
1. Assign digits to the outcomes so the probabilities match.
2. Decide what one trial is.
3. Run many trials with random digits.
4. Count how often the event happened.		 A basketball player makes 70% of her free throws. About how often does she make all 3 of 3 shots?
1. Digits 0 to 6 = make. 7, 8, 9 = miss.
2. One trial = 3 digits (3 shots).
3. Run 10 trials.
4. 4 of the 10 made all 3: $\frac{4}{10} = 0.4$
Law of large numbers The more trials you do, the closer the experimental probability usually gets to the theoretical probability. The theoretical probability of making all 3 shots is $0.7 \cdot 0.7 \cdot 0.7 = 0.343$. With more trials, the simulation would get closer to it.

Counting

ToolFormulaWhen to useExample
Counting principle One choice has $m$ options. A second choice has $n$ options: $m \cdot n$ ways. Count the ways to make both choices. Also when repeats are allowed. 3 shirts and 4 pairs of pants: $3 \cdot 4 = 12$ outfits
3-letter codes: $26 \cdot 26 \cdot 26 = 17{,}576$
Factorial $n! = n \cdot (n - 1) \cdot (n - 2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1$
Read “$n$ factorial.”   $0! = 1$		 Arrange all $n$ things in order. 4 students sit in 4 chairs in a row: $4! = 24$ ways
Permutation ${}_nP_r = \dfrac{n!}{(n - r)!}$
Read “$n$ permute $r$.”		 Order matters. Arrange $r$ of $n$ things. No repeats. 10 students fill 3 jobs:
${}_{10}P_3 = 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$
3-letter codes: $26 \cdot 25 \cdot 24 = 15{,}600$
Combination ${}_nC_r = \left.\displaystyle\binom{n}{r}\right. = \dfrac{n!}{r!\,\mathopen{(}n - r\mathclose{)}!}$
Read “$n$ choose $r$.”		 Order does not matter. Choose $r$ of $n$ things. A committee of 3 from 5 students: ${}_5C_3 = \frac{5!}{3!\,2!} = 10$

Permutation or combination? Ask: if I switch the order, is it a different result?
