The law of large numbers: the more trials you do, the closer the experimental probability usually gets to the theoretical probability.

A simulation uses random numbers to act out a chance event many times.

1.Assign digits to the outcomes so the probabilities match.
2.Decide what one trial is.
3.Run many trials with random digits.
4.Count how often the event happened.

Example: A basketball player makes 70% of her free throws. About how often does she make all 3 of 3 shots?

1.Digits 0 to 6 = make (7 of the 10 digits is 70%).  Digits 7, 8, 9 = miss.
2.One trial = 3 digits (3 shots).
3.Use row 1 below. Make 10 trials.

2. How would you assign random digits to simulate each event?
  1. A coin lands heads (P=0.5P = 0.5).  0 to 4 = heads, 5 to 9 = tails

  3. A number cube is rolled.  1 to 6 = the number rolled; skip 0, 7, 8, 9
  4. An event with P=0.25P = 0.25.  Use pairs: 00 to 24 = yes, 25 to 99 = no

4. A student flips a coin 50 times and gets 34 heads. She wonders if the coin is fair. (A fair coin lands heads half the time.) A computer simulated 100 sets of 50 flips of a fair coin. The number of heads ranged from 17 to 33. No set had 34 or more heads.
  1. What does the simulation tell you about getting 34 heads with a fair coin?
    It is very unusual. It did not happen once in 100 simulations.
  2. Do you think the coin is fair? Explain.
    Probably not. 34 heads would almost never happen with a fair coin, so the result is statistically significant.
